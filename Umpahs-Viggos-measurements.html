What the two meters are

The Umpah meter is a moving-coil instrument reading 0 to 10 Umps, with a smallest scale division of 0.1 Umps. Because you can judge where the needle sits between two marks, the usual convention is to quote half a division, so ±0.05 Umps.

The Viggo meter is digital, reading 1 to 100 Vigs, and its display changes in steps of 2 Vigs. A digital display cannot show anything between its steps, so the standard IB treatment is ±1 in the last increment — here ±2 Vigs. Half a step is the alternative convention; the toggle on the reading tab lets you compare the two and see how little the final gradient changes.

Suggested sequence

  1. Take a single reading of each meter and check it. Get the number of decimal places right before worrying about anything else.
  2. Work out the percentage uncertainty of each reading at a low setting and at a high setting. Which meter limits you, and where?
  3. Wind the source right down to zero, so nothing at all is being fed to the meter, and read the dial. Then fit the calibration standard, which supplies exactly 5.000 Umps, and read it again. Describe what you have found in the language of systematic error.
  4. Collect six to eight points across the full range, plot Viggos against Umpahs, and find the gradient and intercept with their uncertainties.
  5. Predict what the intercept should be if the meter were perfect, compare it with the one you measured, and account for the difference. Then tick the correction box and watch which of the two results moves.
  6. Explain to a partner why repeating a reading would do nothing at all about the fault you found in step 3, and what would.

Reading the dial well

Find the two labelled marks the needle lies between, count the small divisions past the lower one, then estimate the last fraction by eye. Keep your line of sight square to the face — looking from the side shifts the apparent position, which is parallax, and it is a genuine source of error in real practicals rather than an excuse for a bad reading.

What the graph is really for

A single reading tells you a value. A straight-line graph tells you a relationship, and the intercept is where a constant offset in one variable shows itself. Two experimenters with the same faulty meter will agree with each other perfectly and both be wrong by the same amount; only comparison against something independent — a standard, a theory, another method — exposes it.

Teacher settings (contains the answers)

True relationship: V = 6.5 U + 20, applied to the true Umpah value.

The Umpah meter carries a zero-calibration error of +0.20 Umps, so every dial reading is 0.20 too high. Students plotting raw readings therefore measure V = 6.5 Uread + 18.7: the gradient is untouched, and the intercept falls short of 20 by 6.5 × 0.20 = 1.3.

Source range: true values 0 to 9.80 Umps, so the dial spans 0.2 to 10.0 and the display spans 20 to 84 Vigs. With the slider at zero the dial sits on 0.2, which is the offset shown directly. The calibration standard pins the true value at 5.000, which reads 5.2 on the dial.

Reading tolerances: within 0.05 of the true dial position is marked excellent, within 0.1 acceptable. Entering the corrected value instead of the raw one is detected and named.
