The e/m estimate is only valid when the beam is balanced. Accepted value: 1.76 × 1011 C kg−1.

Investigation

  1. With the heater on, the cathode warms up and releases electrons by thermionic emission. They are accelerated through Va towards the anode and pass through its hole as a narrow beam.
  2. Set a plate potential only. The uniform electric field exerts a constant force eE towards the positive plate, so the beam follows a parabola between the plates and a straight line afterwards. Try reducing Va and note what happens.
  3. Return Vp to zero and set a coil current only. The magnetic force evB is always perpendicular to the velocity, so the beam follows a circular arc in the field. Use the right-hand rule to check the direction, remembering the electron's negative charge.
  4. Now apply both fields. Adjust I until the spot returns to the centre of the screen. The forces are then equal and opposite:
    eE = evB  ⇒  v = E/B
    This velocity selector measures the speed without needing e or m.
  5. The electrons gained their kinetic energy in the gun:
    eVa = ½mv²  ⇒  e/m = v²/(2Va) = E²/(2VaB²)
    Record balance readings at several values of Vp and Va, then calculate e/m.

How the model is simplified

  • Both fields are uniform and confined to the region between the plates. Real fields have fringing edges.
  • The beam is drawn visible and its electrons are shown in slow motion. In an evacuated tube the beam itself cannot be seen; only the glow where it strikes the screen or glass.
  • Motion is non-relativistic. At 5 kV electrons reach about 0.14c, which introduces an error of roughly 1% in e/m.
  • Gravity is ignored: the gravitational force is about 1014 times smaller than the electric force.
  • The beam has no spread, and all electrons leave the cathode with negligible speed.

In 1897 Thomson used this crossed-field method to show that cathode rays were particles with a charge-to-mass ratio over a thousand times larger than that of the hydrogen ion.
