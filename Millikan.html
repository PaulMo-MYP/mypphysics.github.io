How to run the experiment

Work on one drop at a time. The bright drop in the centre is the one you are following; the faint ones are out of focus, as they are in the real apparatus.

  • Find the radius. Switch the plates off and time the drop as it falls a whole number of graticule divisions. This gives the terminal speed v↓, and Stokes' law then gives the radius.
  • Find the charge, method 1 (balancing). Turn the plates on and adjust the p.d. until the drop hangs still, apart from its Brownian jitter. Record that p.d.
  • Find the charge, method 2 (rise and fall). Set any convenient p.d. that drives the drop upwards, then time it over a known distance. You do not need to balance it.
  • Change the charge. Ionise the drop to knock electrons on or off, then measure it again. The same drop with a different charge is strong evidence for quantisation.
  • Collect at least 15 to 20 drops before judging the pattern in the charges.

Which polarity lifts the drop tells you the sign of its charge: a negatively charged drop rises when the top plate is positive.

The physics behind the readings

A drop reaches terminal speed in about 10 µs, so it is always in force balance to the precision you can measure. With the field off, weight minus upthrust equals the viscous drag given by Stokes' law:

(4/3)πr³(ρ_oil − ρ_air)g = 6πηr·v↓ → r = √( 9η·v↓ / (2(ρ_oil − ρ_air)g) )

With the field on and the drop balanced, the electric force carries the whole effective weight:

q·V/d = (4/3)πr³(ρ_oil − ρ_air)g → q = (4/3)πr³(ρ_oil − ρ_air)g·d / V

If instead you let it rise at a steady speed v↑, the drag now acts downwards and the two speeds combine neatly:

q·V/d = (4/3)πr³(ρ_oil − ρ_air)g + 6πηr·v↑ → q = 6πηr(v↓ + v↑)·d / V

For the balancing method the charge depends on the timings quite sharply, since q ∝ r³ and r ∝ √v↓:

q ∝ s^(3/2) · t↓^(−3/2) · V^(−1) Δq/q = 1.5·(Δs/s) + 1.5·(Δt/t) + ΔV/V

A 3% timing error therefore becomes a 4.5% error in the charge, which is why you need many drops rather than one careful one.

Stokes' law and the slip correction

These drops are about a micrometre across and the mean free path in air is about 68 nm, so the air is not quite the smooth continuum Stokes' law assumes. The drop slips between molecules and falls faster than predicted. Cunningham's correction replaces the viscosity with

η_eff = η / (1 + b/(p·r)), b = 8.20 × 10⁻³ Pa m

which is a correction of roughly 8% at r = 1 µm — small, but larger than the scatter in a careful set of readings. Solving for r from a measured v↓ is still exact:

r² + (b/p)·r − r₀² = 0, where r₀ is the uncorrected Stokes radius

The correction is switched off by default in Settings, in both the simulated motion and the analysis, so the two stay consistent. Switching it on in the simulation but leaving it off in the analysis introduces a deliberate systematic error: the drops really do fall faster than Stokes' law says, the analysis attributes that to a larger radius, and the charges come out about 12% high. The measurements look just as tidy as before, and no amount of repetition reveals the fault.

What this simulation does and does not model
  • Drop inertia is neglected: motion is at terminal speed at all times.
  • Brownian motion is modelled, with D = k_BT/(6πηr). It is the main source of random scatter here, exactly as in the real experiment.
  • The drop's own size on screen is not to scale. A real 1 µm drop is far below the resolution of the microscope and is seen as a point of scattered light.
  • Evaporation, convection currents and temperature drift are not modelled. In a real run these limit how long one drop stays usable.
  • Drops are generated only with radii and charges that can be balanced within 0–600 V, which is what an experimenter does by discarding the rest. It means radius and charge are not fully independent here.
  • The view is not inverted. Most real telescopes invert the image, so a drop seen falling is rising.
  • Charges are exact integer multiples of e, so the only scatter you see comes from measurement, not from the physics.
Historical note, and a question for Theory of Knowledge

Millikan and his student Harvey Fletcher published a value of about 1.592 × 10⁻¹⁹ C in 1913, close to but not within experimental error of the modern figure of 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, which is now exact by definition of the ampere. The discrepancy came mostly from a slightly low value for the viscosity of air.

What makes the episode interesting is what happened next. Later measurements crept upwards towards the true value over several decades, rather than scattering around it, which suggests experimenters were reluctant to publish results far from Millikan's. Feynman used this as his example of how a community can take a long time to shake off an authoritative wrong answer.

A second question concerns Millikan's own notebooks, which show that he did not publish every drop he measured. Historians of science disagree over whether this was legitimate rejection of runs he had reason to distrust, or selection towards the answer he expected. Both readings are defensible from the same notebooks.

Worth asking with your own data: when you discard a reading, what exactly is your criterion, and did you fix it before or after you saw the number?
